\section{Introduction}

The task is to assign one of 12 medical specialties to a clinical note described by four fields: \texttt{description}, \texttt{sample\_name}, \texttt{transcription} and \texttt{keywords}. When inspecting the data we found that two properties of the source corpus, MTSamples~\cite{mtsamples}, carry most of the label information: the \texttt{keywords} field usually starts with the name of the specialty, and many notes appear several times with a different specialty in each copy. This led to our research question:

\textbf{RQ:} \emph{How much of the attainable performance comes from these dataset artifacts rather than from the clinical text, and which data augmentation strategy helps the part of the problem that does require reading the text?}

\section{Data}

\subsection{Data Analysis}

\texttt{train.csv} has 2,616 rows. In 3 of them, unescaped line breaks place text fragments in the label column; we discard them, keeping $N=2{,}613$ notes. The test set has 398 notes. Median lengths are 405 words (transcription) and 16 words (description); 30 training notes (2 in test) have an empty transcription.

\textbf{Imbalance.} \spec{Surgery} has 876 notes (33.5\%), followed by \spec{Cardiovascular-Pulmonary} (299) and \spec{Orthopedic} (292); \spec{Psychiatry-Psychology} (40) and \spec{Dermatology} (25) are the smallest classes, a 35:1 ratio. The baseline (Table~\ref{tab:main}) has 0\% F1 in 7 classes.

\textbf{Observation 1: the label is written in \texttt{keywords}.} In 2,172 notes (83.1\%) the first keyword is a specialty name, e.g.\ \texttt{``neurology, temporal lobe, brain tumor, ...''}. It coincides with the gold label in 2,171 of them (the exception is a note labelled \spec{General Medicine} with keyword \texttt{obstetrics / gynecology}). The same pattern occurs in 320 of the 398 test notes. It is the website category of the note: metadata, not clinical content.

\textbf{Observation 2: cross-listed duplicates with conflicting labels.} 1,469 training rows (56\%) share their transcription with at least one other row, forming 702 groups. In 701 of these groups the copies have \emph{different} labels: the website lists an operative report under \spec{Surgery} and also under the organ specialty. The most frequent pairs are \spec{Orthopedic}/\spec{Surgery} (118 groups), \spec{Cardiovascular-Pulmonary}/\spec{Surgery} (113) and \spec{Gastroenterology}/\spec{Surgery} (95). For instance, every note named \texttt{``Carpal Tunnel Release - $k$''} appears once as \spec{Orthopedic} and once as \spec{Surgery}. Both labels are clinically valid, so the task is partly multi-label presented as single-label: from the text alone, the two copies cannot be separated. 237 of the 398 test transcriptions also occur in \texttt{train.csv}.

\subsection{Data Augmentation}

Augmentation is applied only to the training part of each fold (never to evaluated notes). Every class with fewer than 150 training notes (\spec{Obstetrics-Gynecology}, \spec{Neurosurgery}, \spec{Ophthalmology}, \spec{Psychiatry-Psychology} and \spec{Dermatology}) is filled up to 150 notes with synthetic copies of randomly sampled notes of that class. We compare four strategies:
(i)~\textbf{none};
(ii)~\textbf{random oversampling (\ros)}: exact copies;
(iii)~\textbf{word dropout}: copies in which each word of \texttt{description} and \texttt{transcription} is removed with probability 0.15 (the ``random deletion'' operation of EDA~\cite{wei2019eda});
(iv)~\textbf{crossover}: a new transcription formed by the first half of one note and the second half of another note of the same class, which keeps the specialty vocabulary while creating unseen word combinations.
We avoided synonym replacement: general thesauri do not cover clinical terms, and swapping an anatomy term changes the meaning.

\section{Models}

\textbf{Pre-processing.} Each field is lowercased; characters other than letters, digits, ``-'' and ``/'' are replaced by spaces. We do not remove stop words (negations such as ``no'' are relevant in clinical notes) and do not stem, since stemming merges distinct terms (e.g.\ \texttt{neurology}/\texttt{neurologic}). The four fields are concatenated and represented with word uni- and bigram \tfidf (sublinear term frequency, 25,000 most frequent $n$-grams, L2 norm).

\textbf{Baseline.} Multinomial Naive Bayes over \tfidf of \texttt{description} with lowercasing and Porter stemming~\cite{porter1980}, as defined in the task.

\textbf{Text-only classifiers.} To measure what the text alone supports, the specialty term is removed from the head of \texttt{keywords} before training and prediction. We compare three linear classifiers with different inductive biases:
(1)~\textbf{Complement Naive Bayes} (\cnb, $\alpha=0.3$)~\cite{rennie2003cnb}, a generative model designed for imbalanced text, with no class weighting;
(2)~\textbf{Logistic Regression} (\lr, L2, $C=1$), a discriminative probabilistic model;
(3)~\textbf{Linear \svm} (hinge loss, $C=0.1$)~\cite{joachims1998svm}, a maximum-margin model.
\lr and \svm use \texttt{class\_weight=balanced}, i.e.\ the loss of class $c$ is multiplied by $N/(12\,N_c)$.

\textbf{Duplicate rule (artifact 2).} If the transcription of a note to classify is identical to one in the training data, the labels of those training copies are excluded and the classifier returns its highest-scoring \emph{remaining} class. This follows from Observation~2: a copy almost never repeats the label of another copy.

\textbf{Keyword rule (artifact 1).} If the first keyword is a specialty name, it is returned as the prediction.

\textbf{Hybrid system.} Keyword rule when applicable; otherwise text-only classifier with the duplicate rule. This is our final system. For comparison we also train each classifier on the \emph{full} fields (specialty term kept inside the bag of words).

\section{Experimental Setup}

All results are obtained with stratified 5-fold cross-validation (\cv) on the 2,613 training notes (seed 42): the out-of-fold predictions (each note predicted once by a model that did not see it) are our own test set; the duplicate rule only looks up the training folds. \texttt{test\_no\_labels.csv} is used only to produce \texttt{results.txt}. We report accuracy, macro-F1 and minimum per-class F1 (the two task criteria are accuracy $>49\%$ and minimum F1 $>25\%$).

\subsection{Parameters/Hyperparameters}

$C$ (inverse regularisation strength) and $\alpha$ (additive smoothing) were selected on the \cv macro-F1 of the text-only setting among $C\in\{0.05,0.1,0.3,0.5,1,3,10\}$ and $\alpha\in\{0.1,0.3,1\}$. Augmentation target: 150 notes per class; dropout probability 0.15. Implementation: scikit-learn~\cite{sklearn} (LIBLINEAR~\cite{fan2008liblinear} for \svm). The final model is trained on all 2,613 notes with the selected configuration (\svm + \ros).

\section{Discussion}

\textbf{Most common error.} Of the 132 errors of the final system, 80 are notes of other specialties predicted as \spec{General Medicine}, none of them with the specialty keyword. These are consultation notes with the SOAP structure (``chief complaint'', ``history of present illness'', ``review of systems''); this vocabulary is shared by all consultation notes and outweighs the few domain terms. Examples from our out-of-fold test set:

\textbf{Ex.~1} (gold \spec{Cardiovascular-Pulmonary}, predicted \spec{General Medicine}): \emph{``REASON FOR VISIT: I have been asked to see this 63-year-old man with a dilated cardiomyopathy [...] He presents with a chief complaint of heart failure.''} The diagnosis is cardiological, but the referral template dominates. In \tfidf, ``cardiomyopathy'' is one term among many \spec{General Medicine} terms.

\textbf{Ex.~2} (gold \spec{Surgery}, predicted \spec{Obstetrics-Gynecology}): \emph{``Repeat low transverse cervical cesarean section with delivery of a viable female neonate, bilateral tubal ligation and partial salpingectomy.''} We consider the prediction clinically correct; the gold label reflects the website category, not the content. The same applies to a \emph{``dual-chamber pacemaker implantation''} labelled \spec{Surgery} and predicted \spec{Cardiovascular-Pulmonary}.

\textbf{Ex.~3} (gold \spec{Neurosurgery}, predicted \spec{Psychiatry-Psychology}): \emph{``first followup after shunt surgery [...] eight to ten years of progressive gait impairment, cognitive impairment, and decreased bladder control''}. The classifier ranks \spec{Neurology} first, which is plausible for normal-pressure hydrocephalus; since the same note is labelled \spec{Neurology} in the training folds, the duplicate rule excludes it, and the second choice is driven by ``cognitive''. This shows the limit of the rule: it removes the label already used but does not know which specialty the other copy belongs to.

\textbf{Relation to the RQ.} The answer is that most of the measurable performance comes from the artifacts: from the text alone, linear models reach about 47\% accuracy on this split, and 61.5\% on notes without duplicates, while the artifacts raise accuracy to 94.9\%. Both rules use only given inputs and training labels, but neither reflects the ability to read clinical text~\cite{kaufman2012leakage}: on new notes a system would perform closer to the text-only figures. Augmentation is not the bottleneck: with a cost-sensitive loss, synthetic copies add nothing measurable; the limit is the label definition (``where the note was filed''), not the amount of minority-class data.

\section{Future Work}

Since the remaining errors concern the content of the note and not class frequency, we would (i)~weight the sections of the transcription differently (e.g.\ ``diagnosis'' and ``procedure'' over the history template), (ii)~replace \tfidf with a clinical pre-trained encoder to capture terms such as ``cardiomyopathy'' regardless of the surrounding template, and (iii)~evaluate the text-only model with group-aware splits that keep all copies of a note in the same fold, to measure generalisation to unseen notes.
